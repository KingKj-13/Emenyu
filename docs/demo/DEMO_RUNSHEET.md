## The framing (before you hand him anything)

> "Before I show you anything — I don't want your opinion as an owner yet. I want you hungry. You've sat down, you're at the table, you haven't eaten. Talk out loud as you go. If something annoys you, say so — that's the useful part."

Then hand him the phone with the QR already scanned, or let him scan it.

Do **not** say "AI." Say *"your sommelier"* or *"the recommendation."* He sells expertise; a machine that claims to replace it will put his back up.

---

## Beat 1 — The QR (30 seconds)

Let him react. Most people's honest reaction is mild dread — a QR usually means a PDF.

> "Be honest — when you see a QR at a table, what do you expect?"

Let him say it. **Then let the screen answer**, rather than you.

The landing page is genuinely handsome — Wine / Cocktails / Set Menu / Mains / Starters / Sushi. Say nothing while he looks.

## Beat 2 — The menu (1 minute)

Let him browse. Real photography, prices, allergen lines. Say nothing.

If he pauses on an item with the small icons, point once: *"the glass means there's a pairing, the camera means there's a video."*

**Danger:** if he taps Kingklip and the fix isn't in, he gets a bold red on a line-fish. Steer with a question, not a grab: *"you're a steak man or a fish man?"*

## Beat 3 — The video (30 seconds)

Take him to **Springbok Carpaccio** — the one dish with real footage.

Be straight about it:

> "One thing I'll be upfront about — that's the only dish we've shot properly. The rest are placeholders. Shooting your menu is part of onboarding, not something I'd pretend is already done."

Volunteering the limitation before he finds it buys you more credibility than the video does. He *will* find it otherwise.

## Beat 4 — The pairing (2 minutes) ← **the moment that matters**

Open the pairing panel: **"🍷 Your Sommelier recommends."**

Then hand him the argument:

> "You do this for a living. Tell me where it's wrong."

Let him critique it. Do not defend. Write down what he says — that is free product work from someone whose opinion is worth paying for, and letting an expert correct you is the fastest trust you will ever buy.

If he likes it, the frame is:

> "These aren't invented by a machine. They're yours — configured once, then delivered the same way at every table, on a Tuesday, by a waiter who's been here three weeks."

That is the pitch. A sommelier's nightmare is his palate not scaling past the tables he personally works. You are selling *him at every table*, not a robot replacing him.

## Beat 5 — The staff side (3 minutes)

**Waiter app → open a table → the recommendation card.** This is what he'll actually buy.

Point at three things, in this order:
1. The three registers — Professional / Traditional / Luxury. *"Your waiter reads whichever fits the table."*
2. `Confidence 92% · EV R271`
3. **Revenue Opportunity: Current spend → +R295**

> "That's the whole product in one panel. It's not guessing at your food — it's telling a nineteen-year-old what you would have said."

⚠️ **Only open a table whose recommendation you have checked that morning.** The engine has been observed pairing a wine with another wine, with circular reasoning. In front of him that is unrecoverable.

**Kitchen display** — show it briefly, it's clean and impressive. If he asks why wine is on the kitchen screen: *"drinks routing to the bar is roadmap, not built."* Say it plainly.

## Beat 6 — The money (5 minutes) ← **close here**

**Owner dashboard, 30-day view.**

```
🍷 Your Sommelier added to your tickets:  R 5 214
Revenue R24 438 · 24 orders · R1 018 avg · 45 covers · R543 per cover
Top dishes / Bottom dishes
```

Then stop talking about your numbers and ask for his:

> "How many covers on a good Friday? And what's your average per head?"

Do **his** arithmetic, out loud, on paper. A number he supplied is a number he can't argue with. Never quote your own R60,000 — it's your restaurant, not his, and the moment you use a figure he didn't give you, he starts discounting everything.

⚠️ If the acceptance metric still reads **1%**, do not show that line — he'll compute 8-of-24 and catch the contradiction with "+27%."

## Beat 7 — The downside (2 minutes)

Raise the risk before he does. This is the strongest move you have.

> "Three things can happen. Nobody takes the suggestion — you're exactly where you are now, no loss. They take it and like it — you made more on the same table. They take it and don't — and the system comps their next drink automatically."

Then open the code and show him the reward has single-redeem and an expiry:

> "You asked yourself whether staff or regulars could farm it. So did I — one code, one drink, one visit."

Answering the objection he hasn't voiced yet is worth more than any feature.

Frame the comp in **his** language — pour cost, not CAC:
> "A comped glass costs you what, R8 in the bottle? Against a R45 menu price. That's the cheapest apology in hospitality."

## What you're actually asking for

You have zero paying customers. Do **not** try to close a subscription in this meeting.

Ask for the thing that's worth more:

> "Put it on four tables for two weeks. I'll set it up, I'll configure the pairings with you, and it costs you nothing. If the average bill on those four tables doesn't move, I'll take it out myself and you've lost nothing but the QR stickers."

He's a sommelier and a vineyard owner. If it works in his restaurant, he becomes your reference — and his network is every restaurateur and wine buyer in the province. **That is the actual prize here, not his monthly fee.**

## After

Write down every criticism within the hour, while it's exact. His objections are your roadmap, and you will not get feedback of this quality from a stranger again.
